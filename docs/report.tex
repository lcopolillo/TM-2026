\chapter*{Resumo}

\noindent Este trabalho explora e compara m\'{u}ltiplas abordagens de an\'{a}lise de
sentimento bin\'{a}ria (positivo/negativo) aplicadas ao conjunto de dados IMDB Movie
Reviews. O conjunto de treino cont\'{e}m 41\,750 cr\'{i}ticas e o conjunto de teste
2\,000 cr\'{i}ticas, com distribui\c{c}\~{a}o equilibrada entre as duas classes.

Foram implementadas e avaliadas quatro fam\'{i}lias de m\'{e}todos:
(i) ferramentas baseadas em l\'{e}xicos e regras (TextBlob, VADER e Stanza),
com acu\-r\'{a}cias entre 70\% e 83\%;
(ii) um modelo transformador pr\'{e}-treinado sem ajuste fino
(\texttt{distilbert-base-uncased-finetuned-sst-2-english}), que alcan\c{c}ou 90\%;
(iii) um classificador baseado no l\'{e}xico NRC EmoLex com e sem tratamento da
nega\c{c}\~{a}o, atingindo 64--65\%;
(iv) modelos de aprendizagem autom\'{a}tica cl\'{a}ssica (Regress\~{a}o Log\'{i}stica,
Naive Bayes, SVM) com representa\c{c}\~{o}es BoW e TF-IDF, chegando a 90,5\%,
e o DistilBERT com ajuste fino, que obteve 94,8\%;
(v) utiliza\c{c}\~{a}o do modelo de l\'{i}ngua Claude Haiku baseado em instru\c{c}\~{o}es,
que alcan\c{c}ou 95,5\% com uma instru\c{c}\~{a}o orientada ao dom\'{i}nio.

Os resultados mostram que abordagens baseadas em instru\c{c}\~{o}es com modelos de
l\'{i}ngua de grande dimens\~{a}o, sem qualquer treino espec\'{i}fico no conjunto de
dados, atingiram desempenho compar\'{a}vel ou superior ao ajuste fino de modelos
transformadores.

\chapter*{Abstract}

\noindent This work explores and compares multiple approaches to binary sentiment
analysis (positive/negative) applied to the IMDB Movie Reviews dataset.
The training set contains 41\,750 reviews and the test set 2\,000 reviews,
with a balanced class distribution.

Four families of methods were implemented and evaluated:
(i) lexicon- and rule-based tools (TextBlob, VADER, and Stanza), achieving
70--83\% accuracy;
(ii) a pre-trained transformer without fine-tuning
(\texttt{distilbert-base-uncased-finetuned-sst-2-english}), reaching 90\%;
(iii) an NRC EmoLex-based classifier with and without negation handling, reaching
64--65\%;
(iv) classical machine learning models (Logistic Regression, Naive Bayes, SVM)
with BoW and TF-IDF representations reaching 90.5\%, and fine-tuned DistilBERT
achieving 94.8\%;
(v) instruction-based LLM inference using Claude Haiku, which reached 95.5\%
with a domain-aware prompt.

The results demonstrate that instruction-based LLMs, without any task-specific
training, can match or exceed the performance of fine-tuned transformer models
on this dataset.

\chapter{Introduction}
%=======================================================================

\noindent Sentiment analysis is the computational task of identifying and extracting
subjective information from textual data --- most commonly, the overall polarity
(positive or negative) expressed by an author toward a subject.
It has become one of the most actively researched areas in Natural Language
Processing (NLP) and Text Mining, driven by the vast amount of opinionated content
produced daily on social media, review platforms, and news outlets.

Movie review datasets, such as IMDB, present a particularly rich testbed for
sentiment analysis: the reviews are long (average $\approx$175 words),
linguistically varied, and may contain complex constructions such as irony,
negation, and domain-specific terminology.

This report describes the work carried out for the TMCD 2025/2026 assignment on
Sentiment Analysis.
We apply and compare four families of approaches on the IMDB Movie Reviews dataset:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Task 2.1} --- Pre-existing models: lexicon/rule-based tools
        (TextBlob, VADER, Stanza) and a pre-trained transformer (DistilBERT SST-2);
  \item \textbf{Task 2.2} --- A custom lexicon-based classifier using the NRC
        Word-Emotion Association Lexicon, with and without negation handling;
  \item \textbf{Task 2.3} --- Trained machine learning models: classical approaches
        (Logistic Regression, Naive Bayes, SVM) and transformer fine-tuning
        (DistilBERT);
  \item \textbf{Task 2.4} --- Instruction-based LLM inference using Claude Haiku
        with three prompt strategies.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The remainder of this report is structured as follows.
Chapter~\ref{ch:related} reviews related work.
Chapter~\ref{ch:data} describes and characterises the dataset.
Chapter~\ref{ch:methods} presents the methods and experimental setup.
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports and discusses the results.
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} concludes with insights and directions for future work.

%=======================================================================
\chapter{Related Work}\label{ch:related}
%=======================================================================

\noindent Sentiment analysis has been extensively studied over the past two decades.
This chapter reviews the works most relevant to our experimental setup.

\section{Dataset and Task Definition}

\noindent Maas et al.~\cite{maas2011} introduced the IMDB Large Movie Review
Dataset --- the same dataset used in this work --- alongside a framework for
learning unsupervised sentiment word vectors.
Their SVM baseline with bag-of-words features achieved 87.8\% accuracy on
25\,000 test reviews, while their proposed word-vector model reached 88.9\%.
This paper established IMDB as a standard benchmark for binary sentiment
classification and confirmed that long movie reviews are substantially harder
to classify than short texts such as tweets.

\section{Classical Machine Learning Approaches}

\noindent Pang, Lee, and Vaithyanathan~\cite{pang2002} conducted one of the
earliest and most cited studies on machine learning for sentiment classification.
Using movie reviews, they compared Naive Bayes, Maximum Entropy, and SVM with
bag-of-words and unigram/bigram features.
SVMs consistently outperformed the other models, achieving up to 82.9\% accuracy,
and they showed that standard features from topic classification transfer well to
opinion classification.

Pang and Lee~\cite{pang2008} later published a comprehensive survey on opinion
mining and sentiment analysis, cataloguing methods ranging from lexicon-based
classifiers to machine learning models and discussing the importance of
preprocessing choices such as negation handling and feature selection.

\section{Lexicon-based Approaches}

\noindent Hutto and Gilbert~\cite{hutto2014} proposed VADER (Valence Aware
Dictionary and sEntiment Reasoner), a rule-based model specifically designed for
social media text.
VADER incorporates grammatical rules for handling capitalisation, punctuation, and
degree modifiers, achieving strong results on Twitter data.
As our experiments confirm, its performance degrades on long movie reviews, for
which it was not designed.

Mohammad and Turney~\cite{mohammad2013} describe the NRC Word-Emotion Association
Lexicon (EmoLex), which we employ in Task~2.2.
The lexicon contains over 14\,000 English words annotated with binary flags for
eight basic emotions and two polarities.
The authors validated it on tweet-level sentiment tasks, where aggregating word
polarities provided competitive baselines.

\section{Transformer-based Models}

\noindent Devlin et al.~\cite{devlin2019} introduced BERT (Bidirectional Encoder
Representations from Transformers), a pre-trained language model based on
bidirectional self-attention that set new state-of-the-art results across a wide
range of NLP benchmarks.
Fine-tuning BERT for sequence classification typically requires only a small
amount of task-specific data and a few training epochs.

Sanh et al.~\cite{sanh2019} proposed DistilBERT, a distilled version of BERT that
retains 97\% of its language understanding while being 40\% smaller and 60\%
faster.
We use DistilBERT both as a zero-shot baseline (pre-trained on SST-2) and as a
model fine-tuned directly on IMDB training data.

\section{Large Language Models for Sentiment Analysis}

\noindent Brown et al.~\cite{brown2020} demonstrated that GPT-3 can perform
competitively on many NLP tasks using only in-context examples (few-shot
prompting), without any gradient updates.
This established the paradigm of using LLMs as zero/few-shot classifiers via
carefully crafted prompts --- the approach we explore in Task~2.4.

\section{Data Characteristics and Preprocessing Implications}

\noindent Reviews are comparatively long, averaging 178 words for training and
175 words for the test set.
This length has several practical implications for the methods applied:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Lexicon-based tools} (VADER, TextBlob) aggregate polarity signals
        across many tokens, increasing susceptibility to noise from
        sentiment-neutral words.
  \item \textbf{Transformer models} are limited to 512 tokens; reviews exceeding
        this length (approximately 15\% of the dataset) must be truncated.
  \item \textbf{Classical ML models} benefit from richer vocabulary coverage per
        sample, favouring high-dimensional feature spaces such as TF-IDF.
  \item \textbf{LLM inference} was conducted on reviews truncated to 1\,500
        characters to control API cost.
\end{itemize}

%=======================================================================
\chapter{Methods}\label{ch:methods}
%=======================================================================

\noindent All experiments share a common evaluation protocol: models are evaluated
on the 2\,000-review test set (or a stratified 200-sample subset for Task~2.4),
and performance is reported as accuracy, precision, recall, and macro
F$_1$ score.
Results are tracked in \texttt{results/all\_results.csv} and shared utilities are
in \texttt{src/utils.py}.

\section{Preprocessing Pipeline}

\noindent A configurable text preprocessing pipeline, implemented in
\texttt{src/utils.py}, supports the following operations independently:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Lowercasing} --- converts all characters to lowercase.
  \item \textbf{Punctuation removal} --- strips non-alphabetic characters.
  \item \textbf{Stopword removal} --- removes NLTK English stopwords.
  \item \textbf{Lemmatization} --- applies NLTK \texttt{WordNetLemmatizer}.
  \item \textbf{Negation handling} --- after a negation trigger word
        (\textit{not, no, never, n't, nor, neither, hardly, barely, scarcely}),
        appends the suffix \texttt{\_NEG} to the following three tokens,
        making negated contexts distinguishable as separate features.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Task 2.1.1 --- Lexicon- and Rule-based Tools}

\noindent Three off-the-shelf tools were applied to the raw test set without
any task-specific training:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{TextBlob}~\cite{textblob}: computes a polarity score in $[-1, 1]$
        by averaging word-level polarities from a built-in lexicon.
        Polarity $> 0$ is mapped to \texttt{pos}; $\leq 0$ to \texttt{neg}.
  \item \textbf{VADER}~\cite{hutto2014}: a rule-based tool designed for social
        media text producing a compound score in $[-1, 1]$.
        Compound $\geq 0.05$ is mapped to \texttt{pos}, otherwise \texttt{neg}.
  \item \textbf{Stanza}~\cite{qi2020}: applies a neural sentiment pipeline at the
        sentence level; sentence-level scores (1--3 scale) are averaged and
        thresholded at $> 1$ for \texttt{pos}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Task 2.1.2 --- Pre-trained Transformer Baseline}

\noindent We used the Hugging Face \texttt{pipeline} API with
\texttt{distilbert-base-uncased-finetuned-sst-2-english}, a DistilBERT model
fine-tuned on the Stanford Sentiment Treebank (SST-2), without any additional
training.
This serves as a zero-shot transfer baseline.
Reviews were truncated to 512 tokens.

\section{Task 2.2 --- NRC Lexicon Classifier}

\noindent The NRC Word-Emotion Association Lexicon~\cite{mohammad2013} contains
14\,182 English words with binary polarity flags.
Our classifier counts positive- and negative-annotated tokens per review (after
lowercasing and lemmatization) and assigns the majority class; ties break towards
\texttt{neg}.

Two experiments were conducted:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Without negation handling}: raw polarity token counts.
  \item \textbf{With negation handling} (window $= 3$): polarity of tokens within
        three positions of a negation word is flipped before counting.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Task 2.3 --- Classical Machine Learning}

\noindent Classifiers were trained on the full 41\,750-review training set using
\texttt{scikit-learn}; hyperparameter selection used 5-fold cross-validation.

\subsection{Feature representations}

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Bag-of-Words (BoW)}: \texttt{CountVectorizer}, top 10\,000 features.
  \item \textbf{TF-IDF} (unigrams and bigrams): \texttt{TfidfVectorizer},
        top 10\,000 features.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Both representations were built with the preprocessing pipeline
(lowercase, stopword removal, lemmatization).
The best TF-IDF configuration additionally includes negation marking.

\subsection{Classifiers}

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Logistic Regression (LR)}: L2 regularisation, $C \in \{0.1, 1, 10\}$.
  \item \textbf{Multinomial Naive Bayes (NB)}: $\alpha \in \{0.1, 1.0\}$.
  \item \textbf{Linear SVM}: $C \in \{0.1, 1, 10\}$, hinge loss.
\end{itemize}

\section{Task 2.3 --- Transformer Fine-tuning}

\noindent We fine-tuned \texttt{distilbert-base-uncased} on the IMDB training set
using the Hugging Face \texttt{Trainer} API:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Tokenisation: max 512 tokens, truncation, dynamic padding via
        \texttt{DataCollatorWithPadding}.
  \item Training: 3 epochs, learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$, batch size 16,
        weight decay 0.01, seed 42.
  \item Early stopping with patience 1 (monitoring validation accuracy).
  \item Hardware: CPU (MPS back-end disabled due to memory constraints
        on the development machine).
\end{itemize}

\section{Task 2.4 --- Instruction-based LLM Prompting}

\noindent Due to API cost, we evaluated on a \textbf{stratified 200-sample subset}
(100 positive, 100 negative) drawn at random from the test set (seed 42).
The Anthropic API was used with \textbf{claude-haiku-4-5-20251001}
(\texttt{max\_tokens=10}, default temperature).
Each review was truncated to 1\,500 characters.

Three prompt strategies were evaluated (full prompts in Appendix~A):

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Generic}: classify sentiment as ``positive'' or ``negative''.
  \item \textbf{Domain-aware}: adds context that the text is a movie review.
  \item \textbf{Few-shot}: prepends 3 labelled positive and 3 labelled negative
        examples from the training set.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The model response was parsed with a regex matching the first occurrence
of \texttt{positive} or \texttt{negative} (case-insensitive).

meaning they over-predict positive sentiment.
This pattern is expected given that IMDB reviews tend toward expressive, positive
language even in mixed reviews, which lexicon tools mis-classify.

\noindent The NRC classifier achieves 64.6\% without negation and 65.5\% with

annotation.
Second, the classifier is context-free; it counts token polarities regardless of
syntactic structure, discourse position, or emphasis.

Negation handling provides a consistent $+0.9$ percentage-point improvement,
confirming that flipping polarity in a window after negation words is useful but
insufficient to close the gap with learning-based approaches.

\noindent Several observations arise.

\textbf{Feature representation matters more than classifier choice.}
Upgrading from BoW to TF-IDF unigrams gains approximately $+1$ percentage point
for LR, and moving to TF-IDF bigrams a further $+0.8$ pp.
Bigrams capture short phrases (e.g.\ ``not bad'', ``highly recommended'') that
unigrams miss.

\textbf{Negation marking helps.}
The best classical configuration, SVM with TF-IDF bigrams and negation marking,
achieves 90.45\% accuracy --- a $+0.45$ pp improvement over the same configuration
without negation.
This is consistent with the findings of Pang et al.~\cite{pang2002}.

\textbf{Naive Bayes underperforms.}
Multinomial NB with BoW achieves 85.8\%, lower than LR (88.3\%) with the same
features.
The conditional independence assumption in NB is violated by word co-occurrences
in text, and NB does not benefit as strongly from TF-IDF weighting.

\textbf{GridSearchCV confirms the best regularisation.}
A 5-fold grid search selected $C=0.1$ as optimal for the negation SVM (90.4\%),
nearly identical to $C=1$ (90.5\%), indicating that the SVM is not sensitive to
regularisation strength on this dataset.

\noindent The results follow an intuitive ordering.
The NRC lexicon classifier is the weakest (64--65\%), limited by vocabulary
coverage and the absence of contextual understanding.
The generic rule-based tools (VADER, TextBlob) achieve $\approx$70\%; Stanza's

The pre-trained DistilBERT SST-2 baseline reaches 90\% without any IMDB-specific

Nevertheless, the result demonstrates that modern instruction-tuned LLMs can
perform at a level comparable to fine-tuned models without any task-specific
training data.

%=======================================================================
\chapter{Conclusions}\label{ch:conclusions}
%=======================================================================

\noindent This work provided a systematic comparison of eight sentiment analysis
approaches across four paradigms on the IMDB Movie Reviews dataset.
The key findings are:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Lexicon-based tools are fast but limited.}
        TextBlob and VADER reach only 70\% accuracy on long movie reviews.
        Stanza, which applies a neural pipeline, performs substantially better
        (83\%), confirming that even ``rule-based'' tools benefit from neural
        components.

  \item \textbf{The NRC lexicon provides a poor classifier for long-form reviews.}
        Limited vocabulary coverage and the inability to model context restrict
        it to 64--65\% accuracy.
        Negation handling provides consistent but small improvements.

  \item \textbf{Classical ML with TF-IDF and negation is competitive.}
        SVM with TF-IDF bigrams and negation marking achieves 90.5\%, matching
        the pre-trained DistilBERT baseline while being far faster to train and
        deploy.
        These models are interpretable and do not require GPU hardware.

  \item \textbf{Fine-tuned transformers remain the strongest trained baseline.}
        DistilBERT fine-tuned for 3 epochs reaches 94.8\%, a 4.8 pp improvement
        over zero-shot transfer.
        The main practical limitation is computational cost ($\approx$6.5~h on CPU).

  \item \textbf{Instruction-tuned LLMs are remarkably effective.}
        Claude Haiku, without any fine-tuning, reaches 95.5\% with a domain-aware
        prompt --- the highest accuracy in our study.
        Providing domain context is more effective than few-shot examples,
        suggesting that the model already has strong prior knowledge about movie
        review sentiment.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Future Work}

\noindent Several directions could further improve performance:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Full LLM evaluation}: evaluating Claude Haiku on all 2\,000 test
        reviews would allow a statistically robust comparison with fine-tuned models.
  \item \textbf{Advanced feature engineering}: POS-tag filtering, character n-grams,
        or spell correction could boost TF-IDF-based models.
  \item \textbf{Lexicon combination}: combining NRC with SentiWordNet or AFINN
        could improve vocabulary coverage and classification accuracy.
  \item \textbf{Aspect-level analysis}: extending to aspect-level sentiment
        (e.g.\ acting, direction, screenplay) would provide richer insights for
        movie reviews.
  \item \textbf{GPU fine-tuning}: re-running transformer fine-tuning on a GPU
        would enable hyperparameter search and experimentation with larger models
        (BERT-large, RoBERTa).
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Prompt Definitions (Task 2.4)}\label{app:prompts}
%=======================================================================

\noindent The following prompts were used verbatim in the Claude Haiku experiments.
In all cases, review text was truncated to 1\,500 characters before insertion,
and the API was called with \texttt{max\_tokens=10} and default temperature.

\section{Prompt 1 --- Generic}

\begin{lstlisting}
Classify the sentiment of the following text as "positive" or "negative".
Reply with only one word.

Text: {review}
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Prompt 2 --- Domain-aware}

\begin{lstlisting}
You are analyzing movie reviews. Classify the sentiment of the following
movie review as "positive" or "negative".
Reply with only one word.

Review: {review}
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Prompt 3 --- Few-shot}

\begin{lstlisting}
Classify movie review sentiment as "positive" or "negative".

Examples:
[POS] {example_pos_1}
[NEG] {example_neg_1}
[POS] {example_pos_2}
[NEG] {example_neg_2}
[POS] {example_pos_3}
[NEG] {example_neg_3}

Now classify:
{review}

Reply with only one word: positive or negative.
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent The six few-shot examples were sampled from the training set (random
seed~42): 3 positive and 3 negative reviews, each truncated to 300 characters
within the prompt.
